Executive Summary 
 
This report documents the findings of the United States 
Postal Service’s Household Diary Study (HDS) for 
Fiscal Year (FY) 2023. The main objectives of the study 
are to: 
• Measure the types and volumes of mail sent and received 
by U.S. households,  
 
• Track trends in mail usage over time, and 
  
• Compare mail usage by household demographic 
characteristics. 
 
 
The report examines these trends in the context of 
changes and developments in the wider markets for 
communications and package delivery. 
 
Background  
The Household Diary Study survey, fielded 
continuously since 1987, aims to collect information 
about households’ use of the mail and how that use 
changes over time. The information collected includes 
household demographics, lifestyle, attitudes toward 
mail advertising, bill pay ing practices, and use of the 
Internet and other information technologies. 
The FY 2023 report covers Government Fiscal Year 
2023, with comparisons to 2022, 2021, and other years, 
as appropriate. 
 
Overview  
As seen in Table E.1, in 2023, U.S. households received 
94.0 billion pieces of mail and sent 5.2 billion pieces. 
After excluding the double-count of 1.8 billion pieces 
of mail that households sent to each other , households 
sent and received a total of 97.4 billion pieces,  
accounting for 84% of total U.S. mail. The remaining 
16% of U.S. mail (18.8 billion pieces  was sent from 
non-households to non- households (mostly businesses 
and government mail).

In 2023, correspondence mail totaled 13.4 billion 
pieces, accounting for 14% of all mail sent and received 
by households . Historically, volumes declined 
continuously since the early 2000’s, driven by a rapidly 
growing migration of mail correspondence to the 
various electronic alternatives available on the Internet, 
including emails, text, and social media. More recently, 
from 2021 to 2023, correspondence mail volume fell 
almost 10%, driven by  sharp declines i n personal (-
16%) and social (-23%) correspondence and a more 
gradual decline in business  and government 
correspondence (-6%). Businesses and government are 
often limited in th eir ability to communicate 
electronically due to legal or regulatory protocols 
requiring the exclusive use of hard -copy 
correspondence.  
In 2023, households paid 82% of their 
bills electronically and only 15% by mail 
As in the case of correspondence, transaction mail was 
also influenced by the rapid growth of electronic 
transaction alternatives, such as  online payments and 
online presentments of bills and statements. Payments 
started migrating online in 2004 and, within ten years, 
one-third of the volume had already moved online.  
 
The speed of the electronic migration of payments was 
in large part fueled by various promotional campaigns 
by banks and businesses to quickly broaden the ir 
customers’ use of online bill payments. While most 
promotions offered incentives for switching online, 
some companies chose to  impose fees on customers 
who wanted to continue paying by mail . Measured on 
how quickly payments migrated to the Internet, it seems 
clear that those promotional efforts proved to be very 
successful. For instance, as seen in Table E-3, the share 
payments by mail, which by 2013 had already fallen to 
37%, continued to decline steadily over the next ten 
years, falling to just 15% by 2023. In contrast, the rapid 
migration of mail payments to the internet increased the 
share of electronic payments from 59% in 2013 to 82% 
in 2023.

 Chapter 1:  Introduction – Volumes &
 Trends
The U
nited States Postal Service Household Diary 
Study (HDS) Report documents the findings of Fiscal 
Year (FY) 2023 study. The HDS measures the mail sent 
and received by U.S. households, tracks volume trends 
over time, and compares mail usage by households ’ 
demographic characteristics.   
The Survey 
The Household Diary Study survey, fielded 
continuously since 1987, gathers information on 
household’s use of the mail and how usage changes 
over time. The types of information covered by the 
survey include:  
• V olumes and types of mail sent and received,
• Demographics of mail users,
• Attitudes toward mail and advertising,
• Bill payment behavior, and
• Use of the Internet and other information
technologies and its impact on mail usage.
Fin
dings are used to assist in market research, 
forecasting, and strategic planning within the Postal 
Service and can be viewed by the public on the Postal 
Rate Commission website.  
The Survey Consists of Two Parts: 
1) An entry, or recruitment interview, conducted by
phone or Web, collects demographic and attitudinal
information from about 8,500 households.
2) These households then receive a mail diary, which
collects information on the mail the household
sends and receives in a one-week period. Annually, 
about 5,200 households complete the diary.
The data generated by these two instruments are the 
basis of the analysis in this report.  The FY 2023 HDS 
report covers the period from October 2022 through 
September 2023, equivalent to the Government Fiscal   
Year (GFY) used by the Postal Service. Data from FY 
2021 and FY 2022 are also reported on a GFY basis.  
U.S. Postal Service Volumes  
(This section reviews all mail volumes processed by the 
Postal Service, as reported in RPW reports. They include mail 
to/from households as well as business -to-business mail. All 
other sections of the report will focus exclusively on household 
mail.)  
The Household Diary Study examines the 
types, volumes, and uses of mail sent and 
received by households. 
 In 2023, the Postal Service delivered 116.2 billion 
pieces of mail, a decrease of 8.9% from 127.5 billion 
pieces delivered in 2022 (see Table 1.1). The decrease 
was largely due to lower volumes of Marketing Mail 
and First-Class Mail, which fell 11.5% and 6.1%, 
respectively.  
The steep decline in Marketing Mail (consisting mostly 
of advertising materials) reflected a rapid increase in 
commercial mailers’ use of digital and mobile 
advertising as alternatives to direct mail -- a trend that 
was greatly accelerated by the pandemic.  
The decline in FCM was also driven by the migration of 
mail to the Internet, with the latter providing a 
convenient and lower  costing alternative to 
correspondence and transaction mail, such as payments 
and the presentment of bills and statements.  
Competitive mail volume (mostly package products) 
fell 5.6% in 2023, partly reflecting a lingering impact of 
post-pandemic deceleration in e-commerce activity.

Mail Flows  
Mail volume can be broken into four basic flows, based 
on origin and destination. These flows are:  
1) Household to household,
2) Household to non-household,
3) Non-household to household, and
4) Non-household to non-household, which is calculated as
the residual of total RPW volumes less mail sent and

Chapter 2: Profile of Mail Usage gives an analysis of

Chapter 3: Correspondence  examines mail that is 
used solely or primarily to deliver (non- sales-related) 
communications, such as letters and greeting cards.

Chapter 2:  Profile of Mail Usage 
Introduction  
This chapter provides an overview of  demographic 
trends and other factors affecting mail flows and usage, 
as they  are helpful tool s for assessing the causes of 
change in mail volumes . The breakouts introduced 
provide the framework  for much of the analyses in 
subsequent chapters. 
The first section compares and analy zes long- term 
changes in mail volume  relative to relevant national 
demographic changes, such as the U.S. population and 
household formation, and  delivery points . The next 
section examines the demographic characteristics of 
mail users, contrasting higher-mail-volume households 
with lower -volume households. The third section 
details emerging demographic and technological trends 
that will affect the future of mail.  
 
Mail Volume and Demographics 
Table 2.1 illustrates the progression of mail growth over 
the last four decades (with 13 years for the period 2011- 
2023) and compares it  to changes in national 
demographics.  
Over the entire period, from  1981 to 2023, t otal mail 
volume grew from 110 billion pieces to 116.2 billion, 
or 6%, in part reflect ing the long- term growth in 
population and household formation. The last two, 
however, increased at much faster  rates than mail 
volumes, with adult population a nd the number of 
households surging 48% and 60%, respectively . As 
household formation increased, the number of places to 
which the Postal Service deliver ed increased even 
faster, surging 70% between 1981 and 2023. Such 
growth disparities led the Postal Service to incur 
significant financial losses , by deliver ing less mail 
(lowering revenues) to more delivery points (increasing 
costs). 
 
As Table 2.1 indicates, total mail started declining in 
the 2001-2010 decade, falling at an average annual rate 
of 2.1%. The volume continued declining through the 
next period of 2011 to 2023 at an average annual rate of 
3.0%. In contrast, all key demographic factors affecting 
delivery costs  (population, household formation, and 
delivery points ) continued to rise over both periods, 
each growing about 1% annually.  
 
The last two decades saw significant declines in 
mail volumes as household formation and 
delivery points continued to rise 
 
The 1980s were a time of extraordinary growth in mail 
volume, reflecting strong population growth and an 
increasing number of delivery points. Importantly, there 
were also  significant technological innovations that 
helped raise revenue and reduce processing costs. 
Advances in computerized data  management and 
techniques for sorting large amounts of data created a 
fertile climate for Marketing Mail growth. Additionally, 
computerization of financial systems encouraged a 
greater flow of bills and payments through First-Class 
Mail. These innovations, in turn, led the Postal Service 
to introduce work -sharing discounts, encouraging 
mailers to prepare the mail in ways that reduce d total 
system costs for creating and delivering mail. Mailers 
could take advantage of these discounts by sorting their 
mail by Zip code in advance.  
The 1990’s also saw significant growth in mail volume 
(especially in the second half of the decade), spurred by 
a strong economy and postal rates that increased by less 
than inflation. Additionally, in this decade  the U.S. 
economy rapidly embraced information technology and 
integrated the Internet into its business processes. Those 
events, however, also led to a significant migration of 
mail volumes to the Internet which took place in the 
decades that followed.

The 2001-2010 decade saw the start of deteriorating  
mail volumes brought by the rapid rise and ubiquity of 
the Internet as well as significant economic downturns. 
After t he 2001  economic recession and t he terrorist 
attacks on the World Trade Center led to significant 
volume losses for the USPS , a strong economic 
recovery spurred a rebound in Marketing Mail volume 
that reached new highs , surpassing First-Class Mail  
volume for the first time  in 2005. First-Class Mail, 
however, c ontinued to decline, as large volumes of 
transaction and correspondence mail started migrating 
to the Internet . Later, t he 2008 economic recession 
proved to have a  severe impact on all types of mail , 
resulting in a 12.8 percent drop in total mail, the largest 
volume decrease since the Great Depression. When the 
2008 recession was over, it was followed by a slow but 
long recovery that culminated in 2020. 
Between 2011 to 2023, as U.S. population, household 
formation, and delivery points continued to rise, each 
growing about to 1% annually, mail volumes fell at an 
average annual rate of 3.0 %. The decline in mail 
volumes was mostly due to the persistent  outflow of 
First-Class Mail and (to a lesser extent) Marketing Mail 
to the Internet . Importantly, the 2020 COVID-19 
pandemic returned the economy into a recession , 
further contributing to the downturn in both First-Class 
and Marketing Mail volumes.

This section presents breakouts of households by the

Income, Education, and Age 
Historically , mail us age has shown a strong 
correlation with  household income, age, and ( to a 
lesser  extent) education. As T able 2.4 illustrates, 
except for  households earning less than $35K, the 
relationship between income and education was fairly 
consistent.  At the lower end of the income  scale 
(under $35K) , only 22% of households  were college 
graduates , while the  largest percentage (32%)  were 
HS graduates . A t the highest level of income (over 
$100K) the vast majority (68%) of households  had 
college degrees  and only 14% had HS degree s. 
 
While the relationship between income and age can be 
somewhat more complicated, overall, household 
income and age were found to be closely related. More 
specifically, in Table 2.5 we see that, except for 
incomes over $100K, at every other level of income 
the share of households increased as the age of the 
head of household increased. However, at incomes 
over $100K, the majority of household were between 
35 and 54 years old, while the share of households 
over 55 was slightly lower instead of increasing. In 
general, this result reflects the different composition of 
members of the over 55 cohort. The latter typically 
includes a large share of households who are either 
close to or of retirement age and usually earn less than 
$100K. However, it is also true that, by that point, 
mail behavior is pretty well set and older households 
continue to receive similar amounts of advertising 
materials and periodicals. They also continue to 
receive and pay a similar number of bills, even though 
their incomes decline.
